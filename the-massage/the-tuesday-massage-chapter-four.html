The Tuesday Massage

Chapter Four: The Door Closes

Wednesday came. Then Thursday. Then Friday. The week moved. Paul came home. And everything stopped.

The rest of Tuesday was quiet. Chloe left early — not because she had to, but because she needed to think. She needed to process what had happened. She needed to understand the blowjob, the boundary, the way Mark had held her afterwards, the way he had said "I want it to be" and meant it.

She walked home in the rain, her coat hood up, her phone in her pocket, her mind replaying the afternoon on a loop — the oil, the skin, the taste of him, the sound of his voice when he had said her name. She did not text Paul. She did not feel like it. She felt guilty. She felt excited. She felt confused.

She made dinner — pasta, something simple, something she could eat while staring at the wall. She turned on the television. She did not watch it. She thought about Mark. She thought about his wife. She thought about whether she was replacing someone, whether she was filling a gap, whether that made her cruel or kind or just human.

Paul called at eight. "Hey," he said. "How's your week?"

"Fine," she said.

"You sound tired."

"I am."

"Rough day at the home?"

"Yeah. Rough day."

It was not a lie. It was not the truth either. She had not been to the home on Tuesday — she had taken the morning off, claiming a dental appointment, and had gone to Mark's instead. But the home was always rough. The residents were always demanding. The other carers were always tired. So the lie was easy.

"I miss you," Paul said.

"I miss you too."

"Saturday? I'm back Saturday. We can do something. Dinner? The cinema?"

"Yeah. Sounds good."

"I'll pick you up at seven."

"Okay."

She hung up. She sat on the sofa. She thought about the kiss on her doorstep. She thought about Mark's hand on her hip. She thought: *I am going to have to choose.*

Wednesday was wet. Thursday was wet. Friday was wet. Chloe went to work. She went to the home. She made beds. She cleaned rooms. She showered residents. She thought about Mark. She did not see him. She did not text him. She did not call. She waited, because waiting was part of the ritual, because the Tuesday massage was a Tuesday thing, because anything else was unspoken and undefined and dangerous.

Paul came home on Saturday morning. He was tanned, he was cheerful, he was carrying a bag of duty-free and a smile that was genuine and warm and nothing like the smile she had been imagining all week. He kissed her. He held her. He smelt of airport coffee and unfamiliar soap and the road. He was real. He was here. He was hers.

"You look good," he said.

"You look tanned."

"Ibiza," he said. "Sun, sand, Sangria. The usual."

She laughed. She made him coffee. She sat with him while he unpacked. She listened to his stories — the client he had met, the deal he had closed, the restaurant where he had eaten mussels and drunk too much wine. She nodded. She smiled. She asked questions. She was a good girlfriend. She was attentive. She was present.

But she was not there.

She was thinking about Mark. She was thinking about the massage. She was thinking about the way his hand had felt inside her knickers, the way his mouth had felt around him, the way he had looked at her afterwards like she had given him something he had been starving for. She thought: *I can't do this.* She thought: *I have to.* She thought: *What am I doing?*

Saturday evening, they went to the cinema. Paul chose the film — an action movie, loud, predictable, full of explosions. Chloe watched it. She laughed at the right moments. She gasped at the right moments. She did not really see it. She was thinking about Mark. She was thinking about the massage table. She was thinking about Tuesday.

After the film, they went for a drink. Paul was talkative. He was planning. He was talking about a holiday in Greece, about moving in together, about getting a dog. Chloe nodded. She agreed. She let him plan. She did not tell him that she was not sure. She did not tell him that her body was still humming from Tuesday, that her skin was still remembering the oil, that her mouth was still remembering the taste of him.

Sunday was worse. Paul wanted to spend the day together. They went for a walk. They made lunch. They watched television. Paul was affectionate. He touched her arm. He kissed her neck. He tried to initiate sex in the afternoon, on the sofa, while the television was on. She let him. She lay there. She let him move. She closed her eyes and pictured Mark's hands, Mark's mouth, Mark's weight on top of her, the slow, deliberate way he had touched her, the way he had looked at her like she was a map he was learning.

Paul was efficient. Paul was kind. Paul was not Mark.

She did not enjoy it. She felt guilty afterwards. She felt hollow. She lay in the bathroom and ran water over her face and thought: *This is what normal is. This is what I should want.* But she did not want it. She wanted the massage. She wanted the slow build. She wanted the anticipation. She wanted the Tuesday.

Monday came. Paul went back to work — not to Manchester this time, just to the office, just down the road, just close enough that he could text her throughout the day and call her at lunch and ask her what she wanted for dinner. Chloe replied. She smiled. She managed. She was good at managing. She had been managing for three years.

But the managing was harder now. Because now she knew what was on the other side of it. Now she knew that there was something else — slower, deeper, more honest — waiting for her on Tuesday afternoons, in a flat that smelled of lavender and old books and the man who was becoming the reason she got up in the morning.

Tuesday came. She arrived at two. Mark opened the door. He smiled. He stepped aside. The ritual was the same — coat off, shoes off, towel ready, oil on the table. But something was different. The air was lighter. The tension was gone. They had crossed the line on Thursday. Now they were just two people who wanted each other, who knew it, who did not have to pretend anymore.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi."

"How was your weekend?"

"Quiet," he said. "You?"

"Busy."

She did not elaborate. He did not ask. He knew. He had seen the way she had flinched when he had mentioned Saturday, the way she had looked away, the way she had changed the subject. He knew about Paul. He did not press. He did not need to.

The massage was different this time. Not the massage itself — the hip was still tight, the muscle still knotted, the work still slow and deliberate and thorough. But the extras were gone. There was no wandering hand. No exploration. No slow slide of fingers over the edge of knickers, no warm breath against the ear, no whisper. It was just the massage. Just the oil. Just the hip.

Chloe lay on her stomach. She closed her eyes. She waited for the warmth of his hand. She waited for the pressure. She waited for the moment where he would cross the line, where he would touch her somewhere else, somewhere more, somewhere that would make her gasp. But he did not. He stayed on the hip. He stayed on the leg. He stayed professional.

She felt a flash of disappointment. Then she felt relieved. Then she felt guilty. Then she felt angry — at herself, at Paul, at the situation, at the fact that she had to choose at all.

"Mark," she said, after a while.

"Yeah."

"Can we...?" She paused. She bit her lip. "Can we not just do the massage today?"

He stopped. His hand stilled on her hip. "What do you want?" he asked.

"I want... I don't know. I want more. But I don't want to... if Paul..."

"Paul," he said. The name hung in the air. It was flat. It was final. It was the blocker she had been trying to avoid.

"I'm with him," she said. "I live with him. I love him, I think. Or I did. Or..." She trailed off. She could not finish.

Mark was quiet. He withdrew his hand. He sat back on his heels. Chloe turned her head. She looked at him. He was staring at the wall, at the clock, at the rain, at anything but her. He looked disappointed. He looked resigned. He looked like a man who had known this was coming and had hoped it would not.

"We don't have to stop seeing each other," he said, finally. "The massage is fine. The tennis is fine. We can be friends."

"Friends," she said. The word tasted like ash.

"If that's what you want."

"It's not what I want."

"Then what do you want?"

She did not answer. She could not. She turned her face back into the towel. She lay there, motionless, while the rain tapped against the window and Mark sat beside her, his hands empty, his oil untouched, his heart beating slow and steady and sad.

The session ended quietly. She dressed. She left. He did not walk her to the door. He did not kiss her. He did not say anything. The door closed behind her. The lock clicked. She stood in the corridor, her coat wet, her bag heavy, her mind full of noise, and she thought: *I have made a mistake.*

She thought: *I want him back.*

She thought: *But I can't have him.*

Wednesday came. She did not go to Mark's. She texted him — a short message, an apology, a lie about being busy. He replied with one word: "Okay." She read it. She deleted it. She stared at the blank screen. She thought about Paul. She thought about the life she had built, the life that was safe and steady and good and not this. She thought about Tuesday.

She thought: *I am a coward.*

She thought: *But I am safe.*

And she tried, for the rest of the week, to believe it.
