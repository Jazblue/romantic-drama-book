The Tuesday Massage

Chapter Eight: The Secret Life

Tuesday was Mark. Wednesday through Monday were Paul. And the silence between the two was growing louder every week.

The new arrangement settled into a pattern, the way these things always do — not spoken about, not negotiated, just understood. Tuesday was Mark. Tuesday was the massage, the tea, the quiet, the slow, careful rebuilding of something that had been broken before it had properly begun. Wednesday through Monday were Paul. Wednesday through Monday were the flat, the job, the evenings on the sofa, the weekends away, the future that was planned and safe and good.

But the two halves of her life were beginning to bleed into each other.

She started leaving things at Mark's — a spare jumper, a book, a bottle of shampoo. Small things. Insignificant things. Things that made the flat feel less like a carer's visit and more like a second home. Mark did not say anything. He just put them on the shelf, in the drawer, in the space beside the bed where Sarah's things used to be. He did not mind. He liked having pieces of her there. He liked knowing she would come back.

She started texting him during the week — little messages, nothing explicit, just check-ins, just *how's your hip*, just *thinking of you*, just *can't wait for Tuesday*. He replied. Always. Instantly. Like he had been waiting for her message. Like he had been holding his phone, staring at the screen, waiting for her name to appear.

Paul noticed. He could not help but notice. Chloe was distant. She was thoughtful. She was often lost in her own head, staring at the wall, smiling at her phone, disappearing into a world that he could not see and did not understand. He asked her, gently, if something was wrong. She said no. She said she was tired. She said the job was stressful. She said she was fine.

She was lying. But it was not a lie to him. It was a lie to herself. Because if she admitted that she was thinking about Mark, if she admitted that she was texting him, if she admitted that the massage was not just a massage anymore, then she would have to choose. And she was not ready to choose.

So she kept the secret. She kept the two lives in separate boxes — the Paul box and the Mark box — and she carried them both with her, heavy and awkward and dangerous, like two full shopping bags that she was afraid to put down because she did not know which one she needed more.

The massage on Tuesdays changed, slowly, imperceptibly. It became less about the hip and more about the space between them — the silence, the closeness, the oil on her skin, his hands on her back, the way he would linger a little too long on the small of her back, the way she would arch into the touch without meaning to. There was no blowjob. Not anymore. Not since the talk. But there was something else — softer, slower, more intimate. A hand on her waist. A kiss on her shoulder. A forehead resting against hers while the rain tapped against the window.

She wanted more. She always wanted more. But she did not ask. She did not push. She let the tension build, week by week, until it was a living thing between them, breathing and growing and waiting.

One Tuesday, she arrived and found Mark in the kitchen, cooking. He was wearing an apron — a stupid, floral apron that Sarah had bought him, the one with the strawberries on it, the one he had hidden in the back of the cupboard and had not looked at in four years. He was making pasta. The smell of garlic and tomato filled the flat.

"I thought we could eat first," he said. "Before the massage."

She stared at him. She stared at the apron. She stared at the way he moved around the kitchen, clumsy and careful, like he was not used to being seen like this, like he was not used to having someone in his space while he cooked. She felt a swell of emotion — tenderness, mostly, and something deeper, something that scared her.

"You're wearing Sarah's apron," she said.

He froze. He looked down at himself, at the strawberries, at the faded cotton. His face went red. He reached for the string, to untie it, to hide it.

"Leave it," she said.

"I didn't think — "

"Leave it. It's sweet."

He relaxed. He smiled — small, shy, the smile of a man who had been caught doing something tender and had not been rejected for it. "She loved this apron," he said. "She used to wear it every Sunday. Made the best Bolognese in the world."

"Better than mine?"

"Yours is better."

She laughed. She stepped forward. She kissed his cheek. He flinched, slightly, then leaned into it. The apron was rough under her hand. The skin of his cheek was rough too — stubble, warmth, the texture of a man who had been alone for a long time and was learning how to be touched again.

They ate at the table. They talked about nothing — the rain, the pasta, the strawberry apron, the way the light was fading early now that winter was coming. Mark told her stories about Sarah — funny stories, mostly, the kind that make you laugh even when the person is gone. Chloe listened. She asked questions. She wanted to know everything. She wanted to know the woman who had come before her, the woman whose apron he was wearing, the woman whose hands he had learned from.

When they finished eating, he cleared the plates. He made tea. They sat on the sofa. The massage waited. It could wait. For now, there was something more important — the building of a history, the sharing of a life, the slow, careful weaving of two separate stories into something that might, one day, be strong enough to hold them both.

"Do you ever think about her?" she asked.

"Every day."

"Does it bother you? That I'm here?"

"No. It would bother me if you weren't."

She reached out. She took his hand. His hand was warm. It closed around hers. They sat like that for a long time, watching the rain, listening to the clock, not needing to say anything else.

When she left, at six, he walked her to the door. He kissed her — not the quick, careful kiss of before, but a real kiss, slow and deep and full of the things they could not say. She stood in the hallway, her coat on, her bag heavy, her heart full, and she thought: *This is what I want.*

She thought: *But I still can't have it.*

She walked out into the rain. She got into her car. She drove home. Paul was there. He was cooking. He smiled. He kissed her. She kissed him back. She was good at it. She had had practice.

But while she was kissing Paul, she was thinking about Mark's apron. She was thinking about the way he had looked at her across the table. She was thinking about the way he had said *it would bother me if you weren't here*.

She thought: *I am a terrible person.*

She thought: *I don't want to be.*

And she pushed the thought away, like she always did, and smiled at Paul, and said, "Something smells good."
