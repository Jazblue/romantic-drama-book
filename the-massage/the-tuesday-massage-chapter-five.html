The Tuesday Massage

Chapter Five: The Long Weekend

She chose Paul. But the silence between her and Mark grew louder than any argument.

Chloe did not text Mark for three weeks. Not on Tuesday. Not on Thursday. Not on Saturday. She let the massage slip. She let the tennis slip. She let the friendship — the strange, tender, unspoken thing that had been growing since September — shrink back into the space where she had kept it before: polite, professional, safe.

Paul noticed. Of course he noticed. He was not blind. He was observant. He was the kind of man who remembered birthdays and noticed when her mood shifted and asked, gently, what was wrong. But he did not push. He was patient. He gave her space. He assumed she was tired — from the home, from the hip, from the long winter. He did not know about the massage. He did not know about the blowjob. He did not know that she was lying next to him at night, his arm around her waist, and thinking about another man's hands.

She hated herself for it. She really did. Every night, she lay in the dark and thought about Mark — about the oil, the warmth, the way he had looked at her like she was something precious. She thought about the Tuesday after the blowjob, the one where she had asked for more and he had given her nothing, the one where the blocker had been Paul's name hanging in the air like smoke. She thought about the way he had sat on his heels, his hands empty, his face closed, and she had wanted to reach out and touch him and say *I'm sorry, I'm confused, I don't know what I want*.

But she had not. She had dressed. She had left. She had walked home in the rain and tried to forget.

And now she was living in the gap between two men. One safe. One desired. Neither whole.

Paul proposed a long weekend. A cottage in the Cotswolds. Just the two of them. No work. No phone calls. No tennis. No massage. He booked it on a Thursday. He told her on Friday. He was excited. She could hear it in his voice — the brightness, the planning, the hope that this would fix whatever was wrong, that distance and scenery and a weekend away would bring her back.

She said yes. She said yes because she thought it might work. She said yes because she thought if she could just be with Paul, really be with him, without Mark hovering in the background of her mind, then maybe the confusion would clear. Maybe she would remember why she loved him. Maybe she would stop wanting the other thing.

The cottage was small. It was stone, with a thatched roof and a garden that sloped down to a stream. The bedroom had a four-poster bed with curtains that smelled of lavender. The kitchen had an Aga that was still warm from the previous guests. Paul lit a fire. He opened a bottle of wine. He put on music — something soft, something they had danced to once, something that made her chest ache with memory.

They walked on Saturday. They explored the village. They ate lunch at a pub with a low ceiling and a fire that crackled. Paul held her hand. He was affectionate. He was attentive. He was everything she had ever wanted in a man — kind, steady, reliable, going to marry her one day. She looked at him and thought: *This is good.* She looked at him and thought: *This is not enough.*

Saturday night, they made love. It was gentle. It was careful. It was the kind of sex that people have when they love each other but the fire has dimmed — not bad, not great, just... expected. She lay there and thought about Mark's mouth. She thought about the way he had tasted, the way he had looked at her, the way he had held her afterwards and said *I want this*. She felt guilty. She felt dishonest. She felt like she was betraying Paul with a ghost.

Sunday morning, Paul went for a run. She stayed in bed. She stared at the ceiling. She thought about the massage. She thought about Tuesday. She thought about how long it had been since she had felt skin on skin, oil on muscle, the slow, deliberate pressure of hands that knew her body better than she did.

Paul came back. He was flushed. He was smiling. He made breakfast — eggs, toast, coffee. They ate at the table by the window. The sun was out. The stream was loud. It was perfect. It was everything she had ever wanted. And she wanted to go home.

Monday morning, they drove back. The car was quiet. Paul talked about the garden. He talked about the dog they would get. He talked about the future. Chloe nodded. She agreed. She let him plan. She did not tell him that she was not sure. She did not tell him that her mind was not in the car, that it was back in Mark's flat, that it was on the massage table, that it was in the space between Tuesday and Wednesday where everything had been possible.

They got back to the flat. Paul unpacked. He made tea. He sat on the sofa. He turned on the television. Chloe stood in the kitchen. She looked at her phone. She thought about texting Mark. She did not. She thought about calling him. She did not. She thought about going to his flat on Tuesday, unannounced, and knocking on the door and saying *I'm sorry, I made a mistake, I want you*.

She did not do that either.

Tuesday came. She did not go. She texted him — a lie about being busy, about Paul being home, about needing space. He replied with one word: "Okay." She read it. She cried. She did not know why she was crying. She was safe. She was loved. She had everything she had ever wanted. And she was crying because she had chosen safety over desire, because she had chosen Paul over Mark, because she had chosen the known over the unknown and the unknown was the only thing that had ever made her feel alive.

Wednesday came. She went to work. She did her job. She smiled at the residents. She helped with lunch. She cleaned rooms. She thought about Mark. She thought about the massage. She thought about the blowjob. She thought about the way he had looked at her, the way he had held her, the way he had not pushed, the way he had waited.

Thursday came. She did not go to Mark's. She stayed home. Paul was at work. The flat was quiet. She made tea. She sat by the window. She watched the rain. She thought about the cottage. She thought about the fire. She thought about Paul's hand in hers. She thought: *I should be happy.* She thought: *I am not.*

Friday came. Paul came home early. He had a surprise — tickets to a show, dinner at a restaurant she liked, the evening planned, the future planned, the life planned. She smiled. She thanked him. She kissed him. She meant it, mostly. She loved him. She did. But she was not in love with the way she had been, not the way she was with Mark, not the way she had been on the dance floor at the town hall, not the way she had been on the sofa with his hand inside her knickers and his mouth around him and his eyes on her face like she was the only woman in the world.

Saturday came. They went to the show. They ate dinner. They laughed. Paul was charming. He was attentive. He was perfect. She held his hand. She let him kiss her. She let him plan. She let him love her. She did not tell him that she was thinking about Mark. She did not tell him that she was thinking about the massage. She did not tell him that she was thinking about Tuesday.

Sunday came. Paul went for a run. She stayed in bed. She stared at the ceiling. She thought about Mark. She thought about the massage. She thought about the blowjob. She thought about the way he had looked at her, the way he had held her, the way he had not pushed. She thought: *I have to make a choice.*

She thought: *But I don't want to choose.*

She thought: *I want both.*

And she knew, even as she thought it, that she could not have both. Not really. Not forever. One of them was safe. One of them was desired. And she was the kind of woman who wanted safety, who needed safety, who had built her life around it — and who was discovering, slowly, painfully, that safety was not the same as happiness.

Monday came. Paul went to work. She made tea. She sat by the window. She looked at her phone. She thought about Mark. She thought about Tuesday. She thought about the massage.

She texted him. Three weeks. One message.

"Can we talk?"

He replied instantly. "When?"

"Tuesday."

"Tuesday."

She put the phone down. She stared at the rain. She thought: *I am going to hurt someone.* She thought: *I am going to hurt Paul.* She thought: *I am going to hurt Mark.* She thought: *I am going to hurt myself.*

She thought: *But I have to try.*
