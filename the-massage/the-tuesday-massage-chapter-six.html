The Tuesday Massage

Chapter Six: The Talk

Tuesday came. She knocked. He opened the door. And for the first time, they talked about everything.

Mark had been waiting. Not like a man who paces the floor, not like a man who checks the clock every ten seconds, but like a man who has learned to wait for things that are worth having. He had spent three weeks thinking about the massage, about the blowjob, about the way Chloe had left his flat on that rainy Tuesday, about the silence that had followed, about the text she had sent on Monday morning — three words that had undone him: *Can we talk?*

He had replied instantly. He had not slept much that night. He had lain in his bed, staring at the ceiling, listening to the rain, replaying the last six weeks on a loop — the first massage, the towel, the skin-to-skin moment, the disco, the kiss on the doorstep, the blowjob, the question, the Paul-shaped wall that had appeared out of nowhere and stopped everything cold. He had not blamed her. How could he? He was twice her age. He was married once, long ago, and the marriage had ended badly and he had been alone for twelve years. He was not the kind of man who got to have these things. He was the kind of man who massaged young women for money and went home to an empty flat and listened to the radio until he fell asleep.

But Chloe had changed that. Chloe had walked in with her wet coat and her bad hip and her laughter and her willingness to be touched, and she had changed the shape of his life without even trying. He did not want to lose that. He did not want to go back to the way things were. But he did not want to push her either. He did not want to be the reason she made a mess of the life she had built.

The knock came at two. He opened the door. She was there, wearing jeans and a sweater, her hair damp from the rain, her face open, her eyes bright with something he could not read — fear, maybe, or hope, or both. She looked at him. He looked at her. The silence stretched. Then she stepped inside.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi."

She took off her coat. She took off her shoes. She stood in her socks, looking at him, waiting. The ritual was broken. There was no towel. No oil. No hip to check. Not yet.

"Do you want a drink?" he asked.

"Yes. Tea, please."

He made tea. He carried the mugs to the living room. She sat on the sofa — the same sofa, the one where she had given him the blowjob, the one where he had held her afterwards, the one where everything had changed. She sat carefully, like she was afraid of breaking something. He sat beside her. He did not touch her. Not yet.

"You texted," he said.

"I know."

"Three weeks."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. You had to sort your head out."

"Did you?"

"Sort my head out?"

"Yes."

"No. I knew what I wanted. I just didn't know if you wanted the same."

She was quiet. She stared into her tea. The mug was warm in her hands. The flat was warm. The rain was steady against the window. Outside, the world was grey and soft and forgiving. Inside, there were no more lies.

"I want you to tell me the truth," she said. "About you. About her. About why you're alone."

Mark's throat tightened. He had not expected this. He had expected a conversation about them, about Paul, about whether this was going to happen or not. He had not expected to be asked about his wife.

"Her name was Sarah," he said. "We were married for twenty years. She had cancer. Breast cancer. It came back three times. The last time, it took everything. She died four years ago."

Chloe looked at him. Her eyes were soft. She did not reach out. She did not touch him. She just listened.

"I was a carer for her," he said. "In the end, I was her carer. I washed her. I fed her. I sat with her while she slept. I learned how to touch a body that was broken, how to move it gently, how to find the knots and the tensions and the places where the pain lived. I massaged her every night. It was the only thing that helped."

He paused. He took a breath. He looked at his hands — the hands that had touched Sarah, the hands that had touched Chloe, the hands that were still learning how to touch someone new.

"When she died, I thought I would never touch anyone again. Not like that. Not with that kind of care. I thought the hands were finished."

"But they're not," Chloe said.

"No. They're not."

She put her mug down. She turned to him. She looked at his face — the grey in his hair, the lines around his eyes, the mouth that had been hers on Saturday and hers again on Thursday and that she wanted to be hers forever. She reached out. She touched his hand. His hand was warm. It closed around hers.

"I'm with Paul," she said.

"I know."

"I live with him. I love him. Or I think I do. I'm not sure anymore."

"You don't have to be sure right now."

"But I have to choose."

"Do you?"

"Yes. I can't do this to him. I can't do this to you. I can't..." She trailed off. She bit her lip. "I can't be two people at once."

Mark did not speak. He did not let go of her hand. He just held it, steady, warm, the way he had held Sarah's hand in the hospital, the way he held Chloe's now — not as a lover, not yet, but as a witness. As a man who was listening.

"I want to stay," she said. "I want the massage. I want the disco. I want the kisses. I want..." She stopped. She could not say the rest.

"What?" he asked.

"I want you."

The words were quiet. They were small. They were the bravest thing she had ever said. Mark felt them land in his chest like a stone in still water, sending ripples out in every direction — hope, fear, guilt, longing, the whole messy, beautiful, terrifying spectrum of being alive again after being dead for so long.

"But Paul," he said.

"I know."

"You can't have both."

"I know that too."

She leaned forward. She rested her forehead against his shoulder. She cried — quietly, silently, the way people cry when they have been holding it in for too long, when the dam breaks and there is nothing left to do but let the water run. Mark held her. He did not speak. He just held her, his arm around her shoulders, his hand on her back, his heart beating slow and steady and sad.

They sat like that for a long time. The rain kept tapping. The tea went cold. The light faded. Neither of them moved. Neither of them spoke. They just held each other, two people who had found something rare and fragile and were terrified of breaking it.

"I don't want to lose you," she said, finally.

"You won't."

"How do you know?"

"Because I'm not going anywhere."

She lifted her head. She looked at him. Her eyes were wet. Her face was red. She looked beautiful. She looked broken. She looked like the only person in the world who mattered.

"Can we..." She paused. She took a breath. "Can we start again? Without the massage. Without the expectation. Just... us. Whatever that means."

Mark smiled. It was a small smile. It was sad. It was hopeful. "Yes," he said. "We can try."

She kissed him then — quick, light, the way she had kissed him on the doorstep on Saturday, the way she had kissed him on the sofa on Thursday, but different now, because it was not a tease. It was a promise. It was a beginning.

He kissed her back. His hand was on her cheek. Her hand was on his chest. The kiss was soft. It was careful. It was the kind of kiss that says *I am here, I am staying, I am not going anywhere*.

When they pulled away, she smiled. It was watery. It was real. "I have to go," she said. "Paul is home soon."

"I know."

"I'll see you Tuesday?"

"Tuesday."

She stood. She put on her coat. She opened the door. She looked back at him. "Thank you," she said. "For waiting."

"Always."

She closed the door. He heard her footsteps on the stairs. He heard the front door open and close. He stood in the hallway, the empty mug in his hand, the rain on the window, the silence of the flat, and he thought: *This is the hardest thing I have ever done.*

And he thought: *It is worth it.*
