The Tuesday Massage

Chapter Ten: The Beginning

She had nothing left. And that, for the first time in months, was exactly where she wanted to be.

She slept on the sofa. Mark gave her a blanket and a pillow and left her alone, which was exactly what she needed — not pity, not comfort, just space. The flat was quiet. The rain had stopped. The morning came slowly, grey and soft, through the curtains. She woke up stiff, her hip aching, her eyes puffy, her mouth dry. She sat up. She looked around. She was in Mark's flat. She had no job. She had no home. She had lost Paul. She had lost the life she had built. She had nothing.

And she had never felt more certain.

Mark was in the kitchen. She could smell the coffee. She could hear the radio — Radio 4, low, the shipping forecast, the kind of thing he listened to while he made breakfast. She stood up. She wrapped the blanket around herself. She walked to the kitchen.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi."

"Coffee?"

"Already made."

He handed her a mug. It was chipped — the blue one, the one Sarah had bought, the one he used every morning. She took it. She wrapped her hands around it. The warmth seeped into her fingers. She stood there, in his kitchen, in his apron, in his life, and she thought: *This is where I am supposed to be.*

"I lost everything," she said.

"You didn't lose everything. You lost the thing that wasn't yours."

"Paul was mine."

"Was," he said. "Past tense."

She sipped the coffee. It was strong. It was hot. It was good. She looked at him — at his face, at his hands, at the apron, at the way he stood in his kitchen like he owned it, like he was not afraid of being seen. She thought about the weeks of silence. She thought about the massage. She thought about the blowjob. She thought about the way he had waited, the way he had not pushed, the way he had said *figure it out* and meant it.

"I have to call the home," she said. "Tell them I'm not coming in."

"Do you want me to drive you?"

"No. I need to do it myself."

She went to the bedroom. She closed the door. She sat on the edge of the bed. She dialled the number. The shift supervisor answered. Chloe told her — brief, honest, no excuses. She quit. The supervisor was surprised. She asked if there was another job, another shift, another arrangement. Chloe said no. She said she was leaving. She said she was sorry. She meant it.

She hung up. She sat on the bed. She stared at the wall. She thought about the residents — the old women, the confused men, the woman who sang hymns at lunchtime, the man who always asked for extra sugar. She thought about the job she had hated for three years. She thought about the freedom of not having to go back.

Mark knocked. He opened the door. He was carrying a tray — toast, eggs, more coffee. He set it down on the bed. He sat beside her.

"You okay?" he asked.

"I quit my job."

"Okay."

"I have no money. I have no home. I have nothing."

"You have me."

She looked at him. She reached out. She touched his hand. His hand closed around hers. They sat there, on the edge of the bed, eating toast, drinking coffee, the rain gone, the morning bright, the world outside full of possibilities that they could not yet see.

"What do we do now?" she asked.

"Now," he said, "we take it one day at a time. No secrets. No boxes. No running."

"No Paul."

"No Paul."

She smiled. It was shaky. It was real. It was the first genuine smile she had worn in weeks. She leaned forward. She kissed him — slow, deep, the kind of kiss that says *I am here, I am staying, I am yours*. He kissed her back. His hand was on her cheek. Her hand was on his chest. The tray of toast tipped slightly. Neither of them noticed.

When they pulled away, he said, "I have something to show you."

"What?"

"Come with me."

He led her to the spare room — the room that had been Sarah's office, the room with the desk and the bookshelf and the window that looked out over the garden. He opened the door. He turned on the light. The room was empty, except for a mattress on the floor and a lamp and a stack of books by the bed.

"I cleared it out," he said. "Last week. When you texted. I didn't know if you'd want it. But I thought... if you wanted to stay. For a while. Until you figure things out."

Chloe stared at the room. She stared at the mattress. She stared at the lamp. She stared at the man who had cleared out his dead wife's office to make space for her, who had been waiting for her to text, who had been hoping, quietly, secretly, that she would come.

She turned to him. She kissed him again — fierce now, hungry, the kind of kiss that says *I choose you, I choose this, I choose us*. He held her. He lifted her. He carried her into the room. He laid her on the mattress. He lay down beside her.

The morning light was soft. The room was warm. The world outside was loud and busy and unaware that two people had just started a new life in a flat that smelled of lavender and pasta and the ghost of a woman who had loved this man before Chloe had even been born.

"Tuesday," she said.

"Tuesday," he said.

"Massage?"

"Massage."

"And after?"

"And after."

She smiled. She kissed him. She thought about Paul. She thought about the flat. She thought about the life she had lost. And then she thought about the mattress, the lamp, the man beside her, the Tuesday that was coming, the future that was suddenly, terrifyingly, beautifully possible.

She thought: *I have nothing.*

She thought: *I have everything.*

She thought: *This is the beginning.*

And for the first time in months, she believed it.
